| 3 | Unit 1 · Pre-A1 | say where you're from and where you live now. | `I'm from ___.`<br>`I live in ___ now.` | subject → verb → the rest. | 4 | Unit 1 · Pre-A1 | introduce another person and add one fact about them. | `This is my ___, ___.`<br>`He's from ___.` | `he` / `she` / `they` replace the name once we know who it is. | may assume Core 1–3 | Japanese re-uses the name or drops the pronoun; English pronominalises after first mention. | 5 | Unit 1 · Pre-A1 | work out who an object belongs to. | `Is this your ___?`<br>`Yes, that one's ___.` | to ask with `be`, put `be` first: *This is* → *Is this*. | may assume Core 1–4 | Japanese questions add a final particle and change nothing else; English **moves the verb**. `This is your bag?` with rising intonation is the predicted form — understandable, but it is the habit that later blocks do-support. |
| 6 | Unit 1 · Pre-A1 | ask what someone is, and answer briefly. | `Are you a ___?`<br>`No, I'm not. I'm a ___.` | short answers repeat the verb: `Yes, I am` / `No, I'm not`. Not just `yes`. | may assume Core 1–5 | bare `yes`/`no` sounds curt in English, and Japanese has no equivalent of the echoed auxiliary. Also the first place to warn about negative questions — see 41. |
| 7 | Unit 2 · Pre-A1 → A1 | say whether something exists nearby and where it is. | `There's a ___ near here.`<br>`It's next to ___.` | `there` here is not a place. It is the word English uses to say something exists. | may assume Core 1–6 | `ある`/`いる` needs no dummy subject, so `Near here a café is` is predicted. Teach `there is` as one unanalysed chunk first; the structure can wait. |
| 8 | Unit 2 · Pre-A1 → A1 | comment on the weather and say what time it is. | `It's ___ today.`<br>`It's about ___.` | this `it` is not a thing. This is pure grammar with no semantic hook — it needs its own slot. |
| 9 | Unit 2 · Pre-A1 → A1 | order or count more than one of something. | `Two ___s, please.`<br>`Just one ___, thanks.` | more than one takes `-s`. It is not optional, even when the number is already there. | may assume Core 1–8 | Japanese does not obligatorily mark plural — `二つの本` marks number once, not twice. So `two coffee` and `many thing` are predicted, and they persist because the number already carried the meaning. Drill the redundancy, since that is exactly what feels wrong to the learner. |
| 10 | Unit 2 · Pre-A1 → A1 | mention something new, then refer back to it. | `There's a ___ on the corner.`<br>`The ___ ___s at ___.` | `a` introduces it. `the` points at the one you both now have in mind. | may assume Core 1–9 | **the core article lesson.** Japanese marks this contrast with `は`/`が` and word order, none of which surfaces as a word — so the learner has the *concept* and no slot to put it in. Teach it as first-mention → later-mention across two sentences, never as a rule about nouns. |
| 11 | Unit 2 · Pre-A1 → A1 | ask where a place is and understand the answer. | `Where's the ___?`<br>`It's behind the ___.` | place words come with the noun: `at` the station, `in` the building, `on` the corner. | may assume Core 1–10 | Japanese `に`/`で` map onto several English prepositions depending on the verb, so a one-to-one substitution fails. Store each preposition **attached to its noun**, never as a list. |
| 12 | Unit 3 · A1 | say what you do regularly and when. | `I start work at ___.`<br>`I usually ___ around ___.` | frequency words go before the verb: `I **usually** start`. | may assume Core 1–11 | adverb placement is freer in Japanese; `I start usually` is predicted. Low cost, worth one correction rather than a drill. |
| 13 | Unit 3 · A1 | ask someone about a habit and answer. | `Do you ___?`<br>`Yes, I do. ___.` | to ask, put `do` at the front and leave the verb alone. | may assume Core 1–12 | **do-support as an operation, and it is genuinely alien.** Japanese builds questions with a final particle; nothing is inserted and nothing moves. `You drink coffee?` is predicted, and it is the single most persistent A1 error. Teach the *operation*, and drill it with the answer form `Yes, I do` so the auxiliary is heard twice. |
| 14 | Unit 3 · A1 | contrast something you do with something you don't. | `I don't ___.`<br>`I ___ instead.` | `don't` + the plain verb. Never `don't drives`. | may assume Core 1–13 | the same insertion as 13, now in the negative. Japanese negates by inflecting the verb itself, so `I no drive` and `I not drive` are predicted. |
| 15 | Unit 3 · A1 | exchange information about a third person. | `She works ___.`<br>`Does she ___?` | he/she/it adds `-s`. In the question the `-s` moves onto `does`, so the verb is plain again: `Does she **like**`. | may assume Core 1–14 | no agreement exists in Japanese, so `She work` and `Does she likes` are both predicted — the second more stubbornly, because it looks like the rule being applied. |
| 16 | Unit 3 · A1 | ask when and where a routine happens. | `What time do you ___?`<br>`Where do you usually ___?` | question word first, then the same `do` question. | may assume Core 1–15 | Japanese leaves the question word in place, so `You get up what time?` is predicted. This is the word-order habit from 3 resurfacing under load, which is why it is re-entered here. |
| 17 | Unit 3 · A1 | ask how often something happens and answer approximately. | `How often do you ___?`<br>`About ___ a week.` | `twice a week`, `three times a month` — `a` here means *per*. | may assume Core 1–16 | another article use with no Japanese counterpart, and it hides inside a frequency phrase. Teach the whole phrase as a chunk. |
| 18 | Unit 3 · A1 | say you don't know, and ask someone who might. | `I'm not sure ___.`<br>`Do you know where ___?` | after `Do you know where`, the order goes back to normal: `where **it is**`, not `where is it`. | may assume Core 1–17 | this is easy for a Japanese speaker to *hear* and hard to produce, because English asks them to un-invert something they just learned to invert. Preview only; it is taught properly at 62. |
| 19 | Unit 4 · A1 | ask a price and check what you heard. | `How much is ___?`<br>`Did you say ___ or ___?` | `this one` / `that one` avoids repeating the noun. | may assume Core 1–18 | fifteen/fifty and thirteen/thirty are a listening problem, not a grammar one, and they cost real money. The repair frame is the point of the lesson. |
| 20 | Unit 4 · A1 | make a practical request and answer one. | `Could you help me with ___?`<br>`Sure, I can help with ___.` | a thing takes `with`; an action takes no `with` — *help me **with** the boxes* / *help me **carry** the boxes*. | may assume Core 1–19 | **the politeness install.** Keigo is grammatical; English politeness is lexical and syntactic, so a learner looking for an inflection finds none and produces `Help me` — perfect grammar, blunt English. Also 「手伝う」 hides the with/no-with split entirely, which is the form point above and the hardest thing in the lesson. |
| 21 | Unit 4 · A1 | ask whether something is allowed, and respond. | `Can I ___?`<br>`Sure, ___.` | `Can I` asks about **me**; `Could you` asks about **you**. Different jobs. | may assume Core 1–20 | both are `〜てもいいですか` territory, so the two get confused. Contrast them directly. |
| 22 | Unit 4 · A1 | order politely and choose between options. | `I'd like ___, please.`<br>`I'll take the ___ one.` | `I'd like` is the polite `I want`. `I want` is not rude, but it is flat. | may assume Core 1–21 | `〜がほしい` maps to `I want`, so the learner reaches for the blunt form by default. This is the same politeness gap as 20, in the one place it is most visible to strangers. |
| 23 | Unit 4 · A1 | ask for an amount of something you cannot count. | `___ ___s of ___, please.`<br>`How much ___ do we need?` | some things are not counted directly — you count the container: `a cup of`, `a piece of`, `a bottle of`. | may assume Core 1–22 | Japanese counts everything with a counter, so the *idea* transfers — but **which** English nouns are non-count is unpredictable (`advice`, `information`, `furniture`). Treat it as vocabulary: the countability rides on the word from first contact. |
| 24 | Unit 4 · A1 | ask for repetition or slower speech without stalling. | `Could you ___ again?`<br>`Could you ___ a bit more ___?` | `the` also points at the only one there is — `the manager`, `the bill`, `the time`. | may assume Core 1–23 | **the article thread's second step** — unique reference, not just second mention. Also the most important survival lesson in the unit: a learner who cannot ask for repetition stops talking. |
| 25 | Unit 5 · A1 | say what you are doing at the moment. | `I'm ___ing today.`<br>`What are you ___ing?` | `be` + `-ing` = happening now, not every day. | may assume Core 1–24 | **the ている split, part one.** `〜ている` covers this *and* the resulting state English handles with the perfect. Here the learner meets only the progressive; naming the split now would overload the lesson, so it is planted and resolved at 57. |
| 26 | Unit 5 · A1 | say how today differs from your normal routine. | `I usually ___.`<br>`But today I'm ___ing.` | usually → plain verb. Right now → `be` + `-ing`. The contrast is the lesson. | may assume Core 1–25 | the two English forms have to be told apart by *meaning*, since Japanese would use the same form for both. Drilling them apart is the whole point; drilling them separately is not enough. |
| 27 | Unit 5 · A1 | talk about what you know, want and think right now. | `I don't ___.`<br>`I know ___.` | some verbs describe a state, not an action, and they do not take `-ing`: `know`, `want`, `understand`, `like`, `need`. | may assume Core 1–26 | `分かっている` and `知っている` are literally the `-ている` form, so `I am knowing` and `I am understanding` are *directly* predicted by the L1 — this is not a random error, it is a faithful translation. It needs its own slot for exactly that reason. |
| 28 | Unit 5 · A1 | check availability and agree on a time. | `Are you free on ___?`<br>`Let's meet at ___.` | `on` a day, `at` a time, `in` a month. Learn them with the word. | may assume Core 1–27 | Japanese `に` covers all three, so this is a pure memorisation cost with no rule to derive. Chunk them. |
| 29 | Unit 5 · A1 | state an arrangement you have already made. | `I'm ___ing ___ at ___.`<br>`Are you still ___ing on ___?` | the same `be` + `-ing` also covers a plan that is already arranged. | may assume Core 1–28 | the learner has just been told `-ing` means *now*, so this looks like a contradiction. Present it as *already decided*, not as a future tense — the diary is the mental image. |
| 30 | Unit 6 · A2 | say how you or a place were at some point in the past. | `I was ___ yesterday.`<br>`There were a lot of ___.` | `was` for I/he/she/it, `were` for you/we/they. | may assume Core 1–29 | Japanese has one past form for all persons, so `We was` is predicted. Low-stakes but visible. |
| 31 | Unit 6 · A2 | report one finished action and when it happened. | `We ___ed ___ last night.`<br>`What did you do ___?` | regular verbs add `-ed`. The `-ed` is a sound, not a syllable — `watched` is one beat. | may assume Core 1–30 | the pronunciation note matters more than the spelling one: `ウォッチド` turns one syllable into three. See pronunciation track lesson 3. |
| 32 | Unit 6 · A2 | tell a short account using the most frequent irregular verbs. | `We went to ___ and had ___.`<br>`Then I ___ and came home.` | the twenty most common past verbs are irregular. Learn them as words, not as a rule. | may assume Core 1–31 | nothing transfers and nothing is derivable; this is pure memory load. Keep the set small and high-frequency rather than complete. |
| 33 | Unit 6 · A2 | ask whether something happened and answer. | `Did you ___?`<br>`Yes, we did. It was ___.` | `did` takes the tense, so the verb goes back to plain: `Did you **have**`, not `had`. | may assume Core 1–32 | **do-support again, and the failure is different this time.** `Did you went?` marks tense twice — the learner has understood the rule and applied it too well. Drill the plain verb after `did`, not the question form. |
| 34 | Unit 6 · A2 | give a negative answer about the past and add a reason. | `I didn't ___.`<br>`I had too much ___.` | `didn't` + plain verb. Same trap as 33. | may assume Core 1–33 | `I didn't slept` is predicted for the same reason as `Did you went`. |
| 35 | Unit 6 · A2 | put three finished events in a clear order. | `First we ___, and then we ___.`<br>`In the end, we ___.` | sequence words come at the front of the clause. | may assume Core 1–34 | Japanese chains clauses with `〜て` and can run very long; English prefers shorter sentences with explicit connectors. Expect one enormous sentence and split it. |
| 36 | Unit 6 · A2 | say what was in progress when something else happened. | `I was ___ing when you ___ed.`<br>`While I was ___ing, he ___.` | the long thing takes `was ___ing`; the thing that interrupts takes the plain past. | may assume Core 1–35 | `〜ていた` maps onto this cleanly, so it is one of the easier past lessons — the difficulty is holding two clauses, not the aspect. |
| 37 | Unit 7 · A2 | exchange intentions for the near future. | `I'm going to ___.`<br>`What are you going to ___?` | `going to` = already decided, before now. | may assume Core 1–36 | `〜つもり` maps well. The trap is choosing between this and `will` — settled in 38. |
| 38 | Unit 7 · A2 | offer help on the spot and promise something. | `I'll ___.`<br>`Don't worry, I'll ___ by ___.` | `will` = decided *as you speak*. `going to` = decided earlier. | may assume Core 1–37 | Japanese does not force this distinction, so either form gets used for both. Teach it as *when did you decide*, which is a question the learner can actually answer. |
| 39 | Unit 7 · A2 | warn about something uncertain and offer a fallback. | `I might ___.`<br>`If you ___, I'll ___.` | `might` + plain verb. No `to`, no `-s`. | may assume Core 1–38 | `かもしれない` maps well; the error is `might to be` from over-generalising `have to`. |
| 40 | Unit 7 · A2 | give ordinary advice and say when it becomes necessary. | `You should ___.`<br>`If it gets worse, you need to ___.` | `should` = a good idea. `need to` = it's necessary. | may assume Core 1–39 | `〜たほうがいい` is stronger than `should` feels to a native ear, so learners under-soften. Pair it with the politeness work from Unit 4. |
| 41 | Unit 7 · A2 | distinguish required, optional and forbidden. | `You have to ___.`<br>`But you don't have to ___.` | `don't have to` = you can if you want. `mustn't` = don't do it. These are opposites, not variations. | may assume Core 1–40 | `〜なくてもいい` vs `〜てはいけない` exists, so the concept transfers — but the English surface forms look similar and get swapped. Teach them as opposites, side by side. |
| 42 | Unit 7 · A2 | answer a negative question without being misunderstood. | `Don't you ___?`<br>`No, I don't.` | answer the **fact**, not the question. If you like it, say `Yes` — even though the question was negative. | may assume Core 1–41 | **Japanese agrees with the proposition; English agrees with the fact.** `はい` to 「好きじゃないんですか」 means *"correct, I don't"* — in English that same `Yes` means *"I do"*. Low frequency, but every occurrence is a real misunderstanding rather than an accent. Cheap to fix, so it gets its own slot. |
| 43 | Unit 8 · A2 | describe a thing with two useful details. | `It's a ___, ___ place.`<br>`I'm looking for something ___er.` | adjectives go **before** the noun and do not change for plural: `two small cafés`. | may assume Core 1–42 | pre-modification matches Japanese, so this is easy — but `two smalls cafés` appears from over-applying the plural rule from lesson 9. |
| 44 | Unit 8 · A2 | reject something because it exceeds or falls short of a limit. | `It's too ___ for me.`<br>`This one isn't ___ enough.` | `too` comes before the adjective; `enough` comes after it. | may assume Core 1–43 | `すぎる` maps to `too`, but `too` gets used for plain emphasis (`It's too good!`) where English needs `really`. That is the error worth catching. |
| 45 | Unit 8 · A2 | compare two options on two dimensions. | `This one's ___er than that one.`<br>`It's more ___, but it's ___er.` | short words take `-er`; longer words take `more`. | may assume Core 1–44 | `より` maps cleanly, so the concept is free. The cost is purely which adjectives take which form — treat it as vocabulary. |
| 46 | Unit 8 · A2 | identify the strongest option and state a preference. | `It's the ___est ___ here.`<br>`I prefer ___ to ___.` | superlatives always take `the` — there is only one best. | may assume Core 1–45 | another article that has to be there for a structural reason the learner cannot see. `It's best one` is predicted. |
| 47 | Unit 8 · A2 | describe your reaction and the thing that caused it. | `I'm ___ed because it's ___ing.`<br>`You look ___.` | `-ed` is how **you** feel. `-ing` is what the **thing** is like. | may assume Core 1–46 | Japanese i-adjectives do not split experiencer from stimulus, so `I am boring` is predicted and the social cost is high enough to make it memorable. Worth the slot. |
| 48 | Unit 9 · A2 → B1 | ask whether someone has ever done something and answer. | `Have you ever ___?`<br>`I've never ___, but I'd like to.` | `have` + past participle, for *any time in your life so far*. No date attached. | may assume Core 1–47 | `〜たことがある` maps well and makes this the easiest entry into the perfect. Use it as the doorway rather than starting from form. |
| 49 | Unit 9 · A2 → B1 | say whether something is done, and what's left. | `I've already ___.`<br>`Have you ___ yet?` | `already` for sooner than expected, `yet` for still waiting, `just` for a moment ago. | may assume Core 1–48 | `もう`/`まだ` map onto these, but the word order differs and `yet` only appears in questions and negatives. |
| 50 | Unit 9 · A2 → B1 | move from general experience to one specific occasion. | `Have you been to ___?`<br>`Yes, I went there ___.` | the moment you say **when**, you must switch to the plain past. `I've been there last year` is not possible. | may assume Core 1–49 | **this is the payoff lesson for the ている split, and the single hardest thing in Unit 9.** Japanese does not force a choice here, so both English forms feel interchangeable, and `I have been there yesterday` is the classic output. Teach it as a rule about **time phrases**, which is checkable, rather than about aspect, which is not. |
| 51 | Unit 9 · A2 → B1 | say how long a continuing situation has lasted. | `I've ___ for ___.`<br>`How long have you ___?` | `for` + a length of time. `since` + a starting point. | may assume Core 1–50 | Japanese uses the **present** for this — `五年住んでいます` — so `I live here for five years` is directly predicted by the L1. This and 50 are the two that need the most drilling. |
| 52 | Unit 9 · A2 → B1 | describe recent continuous effort and its effect now. | `I've been ___ing since ___.`<br>`That's why I'm ___.` | use this when the activity itself is the point, not the result. | may assume Core 1–51 | the difference from 51 is subtle even for natives; keep the contrast light and let the `That's why` clause carry the meaning. |
| 53 | Unit 9 · A2 → B1 | report what's finished and what remains. | `We've finished ___ of ___.`<br>`We still need to ___.` | `still need to` for what's left. | may assume Core 1–52 | straightforward; this lesson is where the unit's grammar becomes useful at work, which is what makes the unit worth its length. |
| 54 | Unit 10 · B1 | explain why something happened, from either direction. | `I ___ because ___.`<br>`___, so I ___.` | `because` introduces the reason; `so` introduces the result. | may assume Core 1–53 | `から`/`ので` come *after* the reason clause, English `because` comes *before* it — the clause order flips. Expect `I was sick because I stayed home` from a faithful translation. |
| 55 | Unit 10 · B1 | identify a person or thing by what they do. | `She's the one who ___.`<br>`It's the thing that ___.` | the describing part comes **after** the noun in English. | may assume Core 1–54 | **word order re-entry, and the biggest flip in the language.** Japanese pre-modifies — 「電話した人」 puts the whole clause before the noun. `The called person` and `I bought yesterday the book` are predicted. The *concept* is native (連体修飾), so teach the position, not the idea. |
| 56 | Unit 10 · B1 | acknowledge something good while raising a problem. | `I like ___, but ___.`<br>`Even though ___, ___.` | `but` joins two equal halves; `even though` makes one of them the background. | may assume Core 1–55 | `けど` is used far more freely than `but`, including as a soft sentence-ender. Expect `but` stranded at the end of a sentence. |
| 57 | Unit 10 · B1 | say what will happen under a realistic condition. | `If ___, I'll ___.`<br>`What will you do if ___?` | present tense after `if`, even though it's about the future. Never `if it will rain`. | may assume Core 1–56 | Japanese has several conditional forms (`と`/`ば`/`たら`/`なら`), so learners often *over*-differentiate and hunt for an English equivalent of each. Reassure: one form covers them. |
| 58 | Unit 10 · B1 | say when something will happen, and what would prevent it. | `I'll ___ when I ___.`<br>`Unless ___, I'll ___.` | same rule as 57 — present tense after `when` and `unless`. | may assume Core 1–57 | `unless` has no clean Japanese equivalent and is frequently inverted in meaning. Consider teaching it as `if ___ not` first and only then the single word. |
| 59 | Unit 10 · B1 | explain your purpose and recommend a method. | `I went there to ___.`<br>`The best way to ___ is to ___.` | `to` + plain verb answers *why*. | may assume Core 1–58 | `〜ために` maps well. The error is `for see a client` from mapping `ため` to `for`. |
| 60 | Unit 11 · B1 | make a request at two levels of care. | `Could you ___?`<br>`Would you mind ___ing?` | after `Would you mind`, use the `-ing` form. | may assume Core 1–59 | the concept is entirely familiar from keigo; only the mechanics are new, so this moves fast. Watch for `Would you mind to open`. |
| 61 | Unit 11 · B1 | request permission and answer without ambiguity. | `Do you mind if I ___?`<br>`Not at all, ___.` | `No` means *yes, you can*. The polite answer is `Not at all`, not `Yes`. | may assume Core 1–60 | the same polarity trap as lesson 42, now with a social cost attached. Drill the answer, not the question. |
| 62 | Unit 11 · B1 | ask for information without sounding abrupt. | `Do you know where ___ is?`<br>`Could you tell me when ___?` | after `Do you know where`, the order goes back to normal: `where **it is**`. | may assume Core 1–61 | the un-inversion is the difficulty — English asks the learner to undo the very thing lesson 13 installed. | 63 | Unit 11 · B1 | make a general statement about a category. | `___ is expensive here.`<br>`I don't like ___ ___s.` | to talk about something in general, use the plural with no article — or no article at all for things you can't count. | may assume Core 1–62 | **the article thread's last step, and the counter-intuitive one.** After sixty lessons of *always put an article*, this is where the answer is *no article*. | 64 | Unit 11 · B1 | describe a malfunction and suggest a first check. | `My ___ won't ___.`<br>`Have you tried ___ing?` | `won't` here isn't the future — it means *refuses to*. | may assume Core 1–63 | a non-obvious use of `will` with no Japanese counterpart; teach it as a fixed frame for broken things. |
| 65 | Unit 11 · B1 | describe how something is produced or what happened to it. | `It's made in ___.`<br>`My ___ was stolen.` | use this when who did it doesn't matter or isn't known. | may assume Core 1–64 | Japanese passive often carries a sense of being adversely affected (迷惑の受身), which English passive does not. | 66 | Unit 12 · B1 | discuss what you would do in an imaginary present situation. | `If I had ___, I'd ___.`<br>`What would you do if ___?` | past form after `if`, `would` in the other half — even though it's about now. | may assume Core 1–65 | `〜たら` covers both the real and the unreal conditional, so learners do not feel the switch. | 67 | Unit 12 · B1 | express dissatisfaction with your present situation. | `I wish I had ___.`<br>`I wish I could ___.` | `wish` + past form, for something that isn't true now. | may assume Core 1–66 | `〜ればいいのに` maps well; the error is `I wish I have`. |
| 68 | Unit 12 · B1 | distinguish a strong conclusion from a possibility. | `He must be ___.`<br>`Or he might just be ___.` | `must` here isn't obligation — it means *I'm fairly sure*. | may assume Core 1–67 | `はず` maps to this, but the same word `must` meant obligation in lesson 41. ## Author audit rule

A feature is covered only when the learner uses it to complete the lesson can-do. A mention in
an explanation or optional expression does not count. Contextual and Freetalking material may
reuse a Core pattern or label a deliberately memorised chunk; it must not silently turn a later
Core target into assumed productive grammar.
