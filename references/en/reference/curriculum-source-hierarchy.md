# Curriculum Source Hierarchy and Provenance

**Status:** source-of-truth policy for curriculum authors

For the proposed evidence-first workflow—and an explicit list of what has **not** yet been done—see [`proposed-authoritative-build-method.md`](./proposed-authoritative-build-method.md).

## Short answer

This curriculum did **not** begin with a grammar list and turn each grammar item into a lesson. It began with communicative needs, reusable speaking frames, and the two-pattern lesson architecture. The grammar coverage map was created afterward as a backstage audit: it names the forms already required by the patterns, checks for gaps and premature jumps, and makes level assumptions visible.

That is still the right direction of travel. However, author intuition—including AI-generated intuition—must not be the authority for deciding whether the resulting scope is complete or appropriately leveled. Going forward, the curriculum uses the source hierarchy below.

## What each source is authoritative for

### 1. Council of Europe CEFR and Companion Volume

**Use for:** proficiency outcomes, can-do descriptors, interaction, production, reception, mediation, pragmatic competence, and qualitative level claims.

- [Council of Europe — the CEFR as a non-prescriptive framework](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/introduction-and-context)
- [Council of Europe — CEFR descriptors](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors)
- [Council of Europe — purposes and curriculum use](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/uses-and-objectives)
- [CEFR Companion Volume (2020)](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)

The CEFR is authoritative but language-neutral and intentionally non-prescriptive. It does **not** contain an official English grammar syllabus. It tells us what learners should be able to do and how performance develops; it does not provide a ready-made lesson order.

### 2. English Reference Level Descriptions

**Use for:** the bridge between language-neutral CEFR outcomes and English-specific forms.

- [Council of Europe — Reference Level Descriptions by language](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/reference-level-descriptions)

The Council of Europe explains that Reference Level Descriptions identify the words, grammar, and other forms associated with CEFR competence in a particular language. These descriptions are produced by expert national or institutional teams, not directly by the Council of Europe.

### 3. EAQUALS/British Council Core Inventory for General English

**Use for:** the primary practical baseline for English functions, grammar, discourse markers, topics, and approximate CEFR teaching bands.

- [EAQUALS — Core Inventory resource page](https://www.eaquals.org/resources/the-core-inventory-for-general-english/)
- [EAQUALS/British Council — Core Inventory PDF](https://www.eaquals.org/wp-content/uploads/EAQUALS_British_Council_Core_Curriculum_April2011.pdf)

This is the closest fit for our immediate need. It was developed for adult General English by EAQUALS and the British Council under Brian North's leadership. Its process combined language implied by CEFR descriptors, strong CEFR-aligned school syllabuses, widely used coursebooks, teacher surveys, and input from examination boards. It describes itself as guidance rather than a mandatory syllabus.

Use it as the **coverage floor and first level check**, not as a lesson-by-lesson order.

### 4. Cambridge English Profile and English Grammar Profile

**Use for:** granular, corpus-informed evidence about the grammatical forms and meanings learners competently use at different CEFR levels.

- [Cambridge — English Profile Journal and programme description](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-profile-journal)
- [Cambridge — how the English Grammar Profile was built](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/2015/11/11/introducing-english-grammar-profile-1-building-profile/)
- [Cambridge — how to use, and not misuse, the EGP](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/2015/11/16/introducing-english-grammar-profile-2-whats-use-egp/)
- [Cambridge — criterial features for grammar instruction](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/2021/06/23/using-cefr-criterial-features-for-grammar-instruction/)

English Profile is a research programme registered with the Council of Europe and supported by Cambridge and other partners. The English Grammar Profile is valuable because it separates broad grammar labels into particular forms and meanings and checks them against learner-corpus evidence.

It is not an official Council of Europe syllabus, and Cambridge explicitly cautions that it is a profile rather than a prescribed teaching sequence. Its evidence is also weighted toward exam writing, so speaking readiness must still be checked through performance.

### 5. Spoken-language and lexical evidence

**Use for:** deciding whether a candidate pattern is natural, frequent, retrievable, and useful in conversation.

- [British Council — lexical approach](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/teaching-knowledge-database/d-h/lexical-approach)
- Corpus-informed spoken-English references and reputable learner dictionaries
- Current editions of established adult General English series, used as benchmarks rather than copied source material
- Recorded learner pilots and delayed-retrieval evidence from our own lessons

CEFR alignment cannot tell us by itself whether `Could you help me with ___?` is a better teaching frame than a grammatically equivalent but less retrievable formula. Pattern selection still requires spoken-language evidence and learner testing.

## Decision rule for this curriculum

Use the sources in this order:

1. Select a real-world can-do from learner needs and CEFR communicative descriptors.
2. Check the Core Inventory so the relevant English function and grammar are not missing or implausibly early.
3. Use the English Grammar Profile to check the particular **use**, not only the broad grammar label.
4. Choose two natural speaking patterns that accomplish the can-do.
5. Sequence them after the language needed inside their slots.
6. If sources disagree, use the more conservative level for independent production unless the earlier item is explicitly taught as a bounded lexical chunk.
7. Validate the level through recorded performance and delayed retrieval.

The authoritative sources constrain the curriculum; they do not replace curriculum judgment. No lesson should exist merely because a grammar item appears on a list, and no grammar item should be assigned a level merely because it “feels right.”

## Provenance of the current 72-lesson Core

The current sequence was produced in this order:

1. Reuse the Korean curriculum's two-pattern teaching architecture.
2. Extract high-value situations and functions from the supplied Podolingo curriculum and the Korean tracks.
3. Group those functions into a Core spine, contextual transfer, and Freetalking.
4. Write two reusable speaking frames for each Core can-do.
5. Build the grammar coverage map retrospectively from the language those frames require.
6. Audit the patterns for changeability, retrieval, pair coherence, prerequisites, and difficulty jumps.
7. Separate fixed expressions and learner-facing grammar support from the two primary patterns.

Therefore, [`grammar-coverage-map.md`](./grammar-coverage-map.md) is currently a **working synthesis**, not a transcription of an official CEFR grammar list. Its broad coverage is consistent with established General English syllabuses, but row-level source tagging has not yet been completed.

## Required audit before calling the sequence CEFR-aligned

For every grammar-map row, record:

- the relevant Core Inventory level and item;
- the matching English Grammar Profile form/meaning, where available;
- the CEFR can-do or communicative scale supported by the lesson;
- whether the lesson teaches productive control, a bounded lexical preview, or receptive recognition;
- any reason for intentionally placing the item earlier or later than the reference source.

Give special scrutiny to the B1+ bridge: past modals, past perfect, causatives, future continuous, reported language, and diplomatic workplace frames. Until that row-level audit and learner pilot are complete, `B1+` is a working curriculum band rather than a certification claim.
