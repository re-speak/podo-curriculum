# English Teaching Philosophy and Pattern Standard

This document is the authoring standard for the English curriculum. It explains what the course means by a **pattern**, how patterns become more open as learners advance, and how grammar, expressions, vocabulary, and pronunciation support the two main patterns without competing with them.

## 1. Start with something the learner can do

Every lesson is organized around one observable communicative outcome: introduce yourself, repair a misunderstanding, compare two options, explain a delay, or soften a disagreement. Grammar coverage matters, but the learner meets it through useful language rather than as the lesson title or final goal.

The lesson succeeds when the learner uses the two target patterns to complete the can-do in a short exchange.

## 2. Teach two primary patterns

A pattern is a retrievable piece of language with:

- a stable communicative purpose;
- enough fixed language to be remembered and spoken as a scaffold;
- one or more clearly bounded changeable parts;
- several natural completions;
- a level-appropriate amount of work inside its slots.

The changeable part may be a word, phrase, verb phrase, or clause. Slot size is not the test by itself. The question is whether the whole frame remains a useful speaking scaffold at the learner's current level.

Examples:

| Working level | Appropriate frame | Typical slot load |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-A1–A1 | `I'm from ___.` | familiar noun or short noun phrase |
| A1–A2 | `I want to ___.` | familiar action phrase |
| A2 | `I stayed home because ___.` | one short, supported reason clause |
| B1 | `If ___, I'll ___.` | two familiar clauses in a known relationship |
| B2 | `Even though ___, I still think ___.` | concession plus a developed position |
| C1 | `What concerns me most is that ___.` | a proposition or qualified argument |

A clause slot at B2 or C1 may be exactly what makes a pattern valuable. The same openness may overload an A1 learner.

## 3. Distinguish patterns, expressions, and grammar support

Each has a different job.

### Primary pattern

The learner changes a bounded part and uses the resulting frame to complete the lesson task. Both primary patterns are practiced, retrieved without the model, and assessed.

### Supporting expression

A fixed chunk such as `Anything else?`, `Go ahead`, or `long time no see` helps the exchange sound natural. It may be noticed and used, but it does not count as one of the two changeable patterns and is not automatically a separate mastery target.

### Learner-facing grammar support

This is the smallest form explanation needed to control the patterns—for example, “After *would you mind*, use the `-ing` form.” It supports production rather than becoming a third lesson objective.

### Author metadata

CEFR band, grammar tags, dependencies, likely errors, recycling targets, and assessment notes remain backstage. Authors use them to protect the sequence; learners do not have to study the labels.

## 4. Use a level-sensitive pattern test

Before accepting a target pattern, check:

1. **Purpose:** Does the fixed part signal a stable conversation move?
2. **Retrievability:** Is there enough literal language to remember and say?
3. **Boundaries:** Can the learner tell what may change and what stays fixed?
4. **Range:** Can the learner produce at least four natural, useful completions?
5. **Transfer:** Can the frame work with new details or in more than one closely related situation?
6. **Load:** Are the vocabulary, inflection, and clause-building demands appropriate for this point in the sequence?
7. **Readiness:** Has the language needed inside the slot already been learned, or is it deliberately and lightly scaffolded here?

`Would you mind ___ing?` passes at B1 when the `-ing` choice is supported. `We ___ed ___ last night` is weaker: so little meaningful language remains fixed that it behaves more like a past-tense formula than a retrievable speaking frame.

## 5. Make the pair cooperate

The two patterns should belong in the same small exchange. Strong pairings include:

- question + answer;
- request + response;
- problem + repair;
- statement + follow-up;
- position + reason or contrast;
- event + background;
- proposal + condition.

Sharing a grammar label is not enough. The learner should need Pattern B because Pattern A was used.

## 6. Let complexity grow in the slots

Progression is not simply a march through grammar topics. It is a controlled increase in what learners can place inside familiar conversational scaffolds.

- Early patterns use concrete noun phrases and familiar actions.
- A2 patterns connect short events, reasons, plans, and comparisons.
- B1 patterns organize conditions, explanations, reported information, and interactional repair.
- B2–C1 patterns organize positions, concessions, qualifications, and extended arguments.

An advanced pattern may retain a short fixed anchor while accepting a complex proposition. An early pattern should normally constrain both vocabulary and structure more tightly.

## 7. Recycle before expanding

Core is the main inventory. Contextual English should normally reuse a Core pattern exactly or make one bounded expansion whose new work is transparent. Freetalking should retrieve known patterns and conversation moves rather than quietly introduce a new grammar syllabus.

Useful recycling has three stages:

1. same frame, new vocabulary;
2. same communicative anchor, one controlled expansion;
3. freer combination with other known frames.

When a contextual or freetalking lesson requires a later structure, move the lesson, simplify the frame, or name the higher prerequisite. Do not hide the jump.

## 8. Keep the teaching hierarchy visible

A complete lesson may contain:

1. one can-do;
2. Pattern A;
3. Pattern B;
4. one or two supporting expressions;
5. one focused grammar-support point, if needed;
6. a small vocabulary bank for the slots;
7. an optional sound cue when it improves intelligibility;
8. one final exchange that requires both patterns.

Only the two patterns are guaranteed production targets. Supporting material earns its place by helping the learner understand, control, or use them.

## 9. Assess communication through the patterns

The quick check asks whether the learner:

- understands the purpose of both patterns;
- changes the slots without losing the frame;
- controls the supporting grammar well enough to be understood;
- combines both patterns to complete the can-do;
- retrieves them after the written model is removed.

Expression use, grammatical terminology, and accent imitation are not separate success conditions unless the lesson explicitly makes one essential to the real-world outcome.

## 10. Treat levels as working claims

CEFR and English Grammar Profile evidence help authors judge readiness, but neither is a universal acquisition script. A level label is a planning claim that must ultimately be checked through learner performance, delayed retrieval, and successful transfer.

When evidence is uncertain, prefer the more teachable sequence: reduce simultaneous novelty, preserve useful chunks, and give learners repeated success with known frames before increasing slot openness.
